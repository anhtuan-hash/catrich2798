Objectives and learning products

  • Listen for key ideas and details in two NASA videos. Aim for two correct answers out of three per video or explain a correction using a detail from the clip.
  • Read a scientific source and a classroom chart. Explain what each source can support.
  • Write three English labels of about 25–40 words each. Include a named source, x/N and percentage, a limit on the survey and a practical action.
  • Every group delivers a 45-second pitch to a partner. Everyone speaks. Two groups also present to the class.

90-minute lesson sequence

1. Climate Detective Period 1 · 00–05 · 5 min · Appendix A · I1-A

Which action could our school realistically take, and why?

Complete I1-A independently in one minute. Compare an empty classroom with the lights on and a school using disposable cups. Agree on one problem and one reason in English.

Procedure. Set the task (1 min). Show the two cards and the driving question. Work (2 min). In a new class, students complete I1-A independently for 1 minute, then agree on a problem and reason. Report (1 min). Two groups share. Review (1 min). Introduce the museum brief and distinguish the scenario cards from school observations.

Deliverable. One problem and one supporting reason, such as “Lights left on in an empty room could waste electricity.”

2. Language Mini-Lesson Period 1 · 05–15 · 10 min · Appendix A · A1–A3

What traps heat? What adds more CO₂?

Watch NASA video 1. Note two key words and answer A1–A3 as a group. Cite NASA in one English sentence explaining cause and effect.

Procedure. Set the task (1 min). Ask “What traps heat? What adds more CO₂?” Work (5 min). Students note two key words, replay the relevant passage or consult the source card, and agree on three answers. Report (2 min). Ask two groups which words helped them, then reveal and explain the answers. Review (2 min). Students write a sentence citing NASA. Groups scoring below 2/3 correct one answer and identify the detail that supports it.

Deliverable. Three group answers and one cause-and-effect sentence with a source. For example, “According to NASA, burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, so more heat can be trapped.”

NASA: What Is the Greenhouse Effect? · YouTube · Transcript

Play a short teacher-selected excerpt during Activity 2, not the entire video if time is limited.

  1. What surrounds Earth and contains heat-trapping gases?
    1. A glass roof
    2. The atmosphere
    3. A layer of ice
  2. Which human activity releases extra carbon dioxide?
    1. Burning coal and oil
    2. Watching the weather
    3. Turning off unused lights
  3. Which sentence correctly reports NASA’s explanation?
    1. Our school survey measures emissions.
    2. Burning fossil fuels adds CO₂, so more heat can be trapped.
    3. Greenhouse gases stop all sunlight.
Read-aloud or source card · Appendix A

Earth is surrounded by a layer of gases called the atmosphere. Sunlight warms Earth’s surface. The surface releases heat, and some gases in the atmosphere hold back part of that heat. Carbon dioxide is one of these gases. Burning fossil fuels such as coal and oil puts more carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. With more heat-trapping gases, more heat can be held near the surface, and Earth can become warmer.

Teacher answer key
  1. B · NASA describes the atmosphere as a jacket of gases around Earth.
  2. A · The NASA video identifies burning fossil fuels as a source of extra carbon dioxide.
  3. B · This sentence identifies NASA as the source and connects the cause to the possible result without overstating the evidence.
3. Live Classroom Survey Period 1 · 15–25 · 10 min · B1

As a group, choose ONE action you would prioritise at school.

Cast one priority vote per group. Read the frozen count x, total valid group votes N, percentage and date. In B1, write one sentence reporting the result and one explaining what the votes represent.

Procedure. Explain one vote per group (1 min). Collect one choice and a group code from each team (4 min). Show the completed BRIAN chart or tally on the board (3 min). Have teams write a result and its limit in B1 (2 min). On paper, add the valid votes and use 100 × x/N for each share.

Deliverable. An automatically generated chart or frequency table. Each group records the date, x, N and the displayed percentage and writes two English sentences reporting the result and its limit.

4. Problem–Evidence–Solution Period 1 · 25–37 · 12 min · Appendix A · B1–B3 and Worksheet B2

Are global warming and climate change the same?

Watch NASA video 2 and answer its three questions. Use B2 to compare two ways of presenting the same action. Score clarity, feasibility and source traceability from 0 to 2. Explain the choice.

Procedure. Pose the question “Are global warming and climate change the same?” (1 min). Play the clip, collect three answers and have teams state the distinction in one English sentence (3 min). Teams enter one NASA fact, one survey finding and two design ideas in B2 (4 min). Ask a neighbouring group which is clearer and why (2 min). Score the designs, choose one and write the reason (2 min).

Deliverable. Three answers, one sentence linking the two concepts and a canvas comparing two options and justifying the selected one.

NASA: Is Climate Change the Same as Global Warming? — We Asked a NASA Expert · YouTube · Transcript

Show the complete clip (about 1 minute 14 seconds), or use 0:10–1:06 if time is limited.

  1. What does global warming refer to?
    1. A long-term rise in Earth’s average surface temperature
    2. One unusually hot day
    3. The temperature inside a classroom
  2. Which example is mentioned as an effect of broader climate change?
    1. More sea ice in the Arctic
    2. Decreasing Arctic ice and droughts
    3. A vote about school recycling
  3. Which statement responsibly connects NASA evidence to our museum project?
    1. Our classroom vote proves warming has stopped.
    2. The two terms mean exactly the same thing.
    3. Global warming is part of climate change. The survey records group priorities.
Read-aloud or source card · Appendix A

Global warming is a long-term rise in Earth’s average surface temperature. Climate change is broader. It includes long-term changes in typical conditions across regions and around the world. Examples include less ice in the Arctic and droughts. Changes in average surface temperature are one part of that wider picture.

Teacher answer key
  1. A · NASA defines global warming as the long-term rise in average surface temperature across Earth.
  2. B · NASA mentions decreasing Arctic ice, droughts and wildfires among observed signs of climate change.
  3. C · This distinguishes the scientific source from a limited classroom group-priority survey.
5. Storyboard Checkpoint Period 1 · 37–45 · 8 min · B3

What should a visitor see, read and do?

Use B3 to write one opening sentence per panel. Plan visuals, credits and reading order. Mark the source location and write the expected answers to the three C2 questions.

Procedure. Show a three-panel layout (1 min). Each team writes one opening sentence per panel, marks where the source goes and enters its expected C2 answers on B3 (5 min). Ask two teams to point to the planned source (1 min). Check for missing sources and keep B3 for Period 2 (1 min).

Deliverable. A three-panel storyboard with draft opening lines, poster ideas, sources, assigned roles and a visitor test plan.

6. Recap and Resume Period 2 · 00–04 · 4 min · B3 · C2

Can visitors name the problem, find the source and explain the action?

Reopen B3 and the class chart or paper tally. Confirm roles, recall the three C2 questions and practise keeping a copy of V1.

Procedure. Return B3 and show the class chart or tally (1 min). Teams check their roles and open a draft or fold a blank sheet in thirds (1 min). Ask one group to recall the three C2 questions (1 min). Show how to keep a copy of the first draft before testing (1 min).

Deliverable. An open storyboard, confirmed roles and an 18-minute target for producing V1.

7. Create the Museum Period 2 · 04–22 · 18 min · B3 · C1 · Appendix K when using image generation

Build the first version of your museum.

Build three English labels of 25–40 words. Place the chart or tally beside The Evidence. Link the source next to the claim. Credit each visual, add alt text to digital images, and save V1 with one sentence about a check.

Procedure. Start · 2 min. Open the template or use the folded sheet. Show where the source and tally go.
Build · 12 min. Write the labels and check the figures in the first 5 minutes. Use the next 7 minutes to add visuals, credits and the class tally. Use a drawing or a credited visual if the digital image is unfinished.
Check · 4 min. In 3 minutes, have groups check all three panels and name one correction. In the last minute, save a copy or write V1 on the sheet.

Deliverable. One saved or paper copy of V1 and a one-sentence note on what the team checked. If using image generation, keep the English prompt and check the output against the label.

8. Peer Gallery Walk Period 2 · 22–30 · 8 min · C2

Can visitors understand your V1 without help?

Visit one group’s V1. Without help from the authors, answer the three C2 questions in English. Authors check against B3 and mark each answer 0 or 1. The visitors write one specific suggestion.

Procedure. Pair · 1 min. Pair neighbouring teams. With an odd number, have the last three exchange drafts in a circle.
Read · 3 min. Each team views one draft and records its three answers on C2. Authors stay silent.
Check · 2 min. Return each card to its authors. Check the answers against B3 and mark 0 or 1 for each.
Respond · 2 min. Visitors write one suggestion in English, such as “Name who checks the lights.” Authors choose what to change.

Deliverable. One C2 card per group’s V1 museum draft, with three scored answers and one specific suggestion in English.

9. Improve the Exhibition Period 2 · 30–36 · 6 min · C3

Which change will help the visitor understand the exhibit?

Keep V1 and save one reasoned change as V2. Use C3 to repeat only the question for the part that changed. Ask the same visitor group, preferably a different reader. Record the answer and both scores for that question.

Procedure. Choose · 1 min. Choose one suggestion. If all answers were correct, make one answer easier to find.
Revise · 3 min. Keep V1 and save the changed copy as V2. Note what you changed and why on C3.
Retest · 2 min. Return V2 to the team that tested your draft. If possible, ask a different reader to answer the same question without help. Record both scores for that question and who read V2.

Deliverable. V1, V2 and the matched 0/1 result for the revised criterion. Explain the change in English.

10. Present and Discuss Period 2 · 36–42 · 6 min · D1 · E3

Explain your problem, evidence and proposed action.

Every group gives a 45-second pitch to a partner. Everyone says one short sentence. Allow 15 seconds for a question and reply. Hear two examples for the whole class. Record on D1 and prepare the group Exit Ticket.

Procedure. Have teams open V2 and assign lines (1 min). Partners take turns, with 45 seconds to present and 15 seconds for a question and reply per group (2 min). Hear any unpaired team while the pairs present. Then hear two 45-second examples and one class question and reply (2 min). Give feedback and ask each group to draft its Exit Ticket sentence (1 min). Use D1 for the peer record and E3 for contributions you hear.

Deliverable. One pitch per group in pairs, two plenary examples and a recorded question and answer.

11. Exit Ticket Period 2 · 42–45 · 3 min · I1-B · D2

What environmental action will your group take, and why?

Write I1-B independently for one minute. The reporter submits one group sentence beginning “I will … because …” and confirms V2. Track the action in D2 after class.

Procedure. Hand out I1-B and cue the group ticket (30 sec). Students write alone (60 sec). The reporter copies the prepared sentence and confirms V2 (30 sec). Hear one action and its reason (30 sec). Collect the forms and assign D2 (30 sec).

Deliverable. Individual I1-B responses, one group Exit Ticket and the final museum version.

Learning materials and tasks

1 · Understand the problem

Burning fossil fuels adds greenhouse gases to the atmosphere. These gases trap heat and contribute to global warming. A school action can address one part of the problem, but a classroom vote cannot measure how much warming that action prevents.

Underline a cause and its result. Explain the connection using because.

NASA · What Is Climate Change?

2 · Compare feasible actions

Walking or cycling where safe, switching off unused lights and reducing food waste are possible climate actions. Choose an action that fits your school. Explain who will do it, what resources it needs and one practical limitation.

Recommend one action. Explain why it is feasible and identify a limitation.

United Nations · Ten Actions

3 · Read evidence carefully

Our survey records one priority choice per group. N is the number of groups that voted. Report the count, N, percentage and date together. These choices do not show individual habits, represent the whole school or measure emissions saved.

Complete: … out of … groups selected … . We cannot conclude that … .

BRIAN · Original evidence-literacy activity

4 · Write for a visitor

A clear exhibit states a problem, gives relevant evidence and recommends a practical response. Use according to for a source, because for a reason and therefore for a result. Write 25–40 words per exhibit, then revise after a visitor tests the message.

Write three captions. Ask a peer what they learned and what needs clarification.

Language support · Appendix A

Word bank. atmosphere, greenhouse gases, fossil fuels, trap heat, emissions, priority, feasible, source, limitation, revise

Language frames. “According to NASA, …” · “Because …, …” · “In our survey, ___ out of ___ groups (___%) chose …” · “The data describe choices made by the groups in our class.” · “We recommend … because …” · “We changed … after visitors …”

Visitor test · V1 and V2

  1. What is the school problem?
  2. Which source supports one claim? Point to it.
  3. What should someone do? Who does it and when?

On C2, the visitor answers all three questions in three minutes without coaching. Award one point for each correct answer after all three are written. For question 3, the action, person and time must all be present. On C3, repeat only the question for the part you revised. Keep the V1 total out of 3 and the paired scores out of 1. Mark the other V2 items N/T.

Group learning rubric · 100 points

The four levels guide feedback. Use the original recorded score when reviewing a finalized session.

English communication · 40

  1. 40. At least 2/3 per video or corrections with evidence. Three coherent labels and a complete pitch with a reasoned answer.
  2. 32. Understands both main ideas. Readable labels with sources and a proposal. Clear pitch and basic answer.
  3. 24. Partial understanding. Labels lack content or contain errors that obscure meaning. The pitch or answer needs support.
  4. 12. Isolated words or sentences, or the pitch and labels fail to convey the main message.

Evidence & sources · 20

  1. 20. Verifiable NASA claim, correct x, N and percentage, and a clear limit. Conclusion stays within scope.
  2. 16. Both sources are clear and the conclusion is sound. One minor detail needs clarification, such as a unit or rounding note.
  3. 12. Evidence is present, but N or the limitation is missing, or the source is hard to locate.
  4. 6. Unsupported evidence or conclusions outside the scope of the data.

Problem-solving · 15

  1. 15. Specific problem and comparison of two options. The proposal specifies who acts, what they do, when and how to track it.
  2. 12. Two options considered. One implementation detail is missing from an otherwise clear proposal.
  3. 9. Only one option or a general reason. The action needs detail.
  4. 4. The problem or action is vague or poorly connected to evidence.

Prototype & usability · 15

  1. 15. Three readable panels with easy-to-find sources, credited visuals and alt text for digital images.
  2. 12. Three readable panels with visuals. One detail about layout, sources, credit or digital alt text needs attention.
  3. 9. A preview exists but an exhibit, poster or source cue is missing.
  4. 4. The preview is disjointed and visitors struggle to follow it.

Testing & revision · 10

  1. 10. C2 shows a specific barrier or suggestion. V1, V2 and C3 show a reasoned change and a recorded retest of the same item.
  2. 8. A peer check and a reasoned change are documented. The matched retest is missing or incomplete.
  3. 6. Feedback and a revision exist, but the connection is hard to verify.
  4. 3. Only a draft or isolated comment. No documented change based on feedback.

Conclusion and application

A persuasive English exhibit links a clear problem, a source and a feasible recommendation. Group priorities describe our class discussion, not individual behaviour or measured emission reductions. Transfer: choose one feasible action, state who will carry it out and explain how you would check it next week.

Paper-based contingency

Prepare A4 sheets, a timer, B1–B3, C2–C3, D1–D2 and the two individual I1 forms. If groups cannot open drafts at Stage 07, switch to folded A4 panels. If a partial online vote cannot be verified, restart on paper and use that single tally. Keep the original drafts, C2 answers and C3 retest.

After-class action log · D2

Track one approved action for a school week. Record the dates, what you counted and how often the action happened out of the total opportunities. Explain the result and one limitation in two or three English sentences. Submit D2 at the next English lesson after the observation week.
